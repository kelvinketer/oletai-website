Africa's $75 Billion Gap: Why Farmers Without Collateral Can't Access the Capital They Need

K
Kelvin Keter
CEO, Oletai Agro Group

Recent international research published in May 2026 confirmed what millions of farmers have lived for decades: Africa faces a $75 billion annual agricultural financing gap. That is the amount of investment — in tools, inputs, equipment, and services — that farmers need but cannot access. And it is growing.

"The financing gap for smallholder farmers is not a market failure — it is a data failure. Oletai is building the data layer that makes farmers visible to the financial system."

The Root of the Problem

Traditional lenders perceive agriculture as high-risk, discouraged by climate volatility, remote rural locations, and the fragmented scale of smallholder operations. In Kenya, the agriculture sector contributes over 25% of GDP and employs millions — yet it receives less than 5% of total commercial bank lending. Some lenders demand collateral of up to 120% of the loan value, effectively locking out every farmer who does not own titled land.

For the farmer, the barriers are equally formidable. No formal land title means no collateral. Operating in a cash-based economy means no documented credit history. The result is a self-reinforcing cycle: low investment → low yields → deepened perception of risk → less investment.

Knowledge as Collateral

Oletai's answer to this is the Agri-Trust Score — a dynamic creditworthiness measure built from verified agricultural knowledge, not asset ownership. When a farmer completes a module on soil health, irrigation, or pest management, they are not just learning — they are building the profile that unlocks credit. This model directly addresses what development finance research has identified as a primary barrier: the absence of reliable data on smallholder farmers.

The data generated by our biosurveillance network — anonymous, real-time, geospatial — provides lenders with something traditional credit scoring never could: forward-looking agronomic intelligence about specific farms, in specific micro-climates, facing specific risks. This is what makes a farmer bankable without collateral.

The East Africa Opportunity

The African Development Bank President Dr. Akinwumi Adesina stated clearly at the March 2025 Nairobi conference: "Together, let us unleash the potential of agriculture in Africa." His institution is planning a $500 million facility to unlock $10 billion for smallholder farmers — but capital without digital delivery infrastructure reaches only large organisations, not the last mile.

Oletai is building the last-mile layer. Beginning in Kenya — where mobile money penetration is among the highest in the world — and expanding across East Africa, Rahisi Agro Pay connects institutional capital directly to verified, learning farmers through a single smartphone application.

The $75 billion gap is a massive challenge. It is also a massive opportunity. And it starts with data.

Why Young Kenyans Are Leaving the Farm — and How We're Bringing Them Back

E
Eric Kosgey
Co-Founder & Director, Oletai Agro Group

Landmark international research on youth and agriculture identified five structural barriers that push young people away from farming globally. All five are present in Kenya. Understanding them is the first step to reversing the trend.

The Five Barriers

  • Access to knowledge and education — Agricultural curricula are outdated and disconnected from real farming challenges. Young people don't learn modern, profitable farming in school.
  • Access to land — Land insecurity is the number one barrier to young people starting farms. Without land, there is no collateral, and without collateral, no credit.
  • Access to financial resources — MFIs charge interest rates that make borrowing unviable. Banks require documentation that first-time farmers cannot provide.
  • Access to green jobs — Agriculture is not framed as a career pathway for educated young people. Vocational training for farming is rare and under-resourced.
  • Access to markets — Growing supermarket dominance makes market entry nearly impossible for small-scale producers without aggregation support.
"The future of food security in East Africa depends on making farming genuinely attractive to the next generation — not just viable, but aspirational."

What Oletai Addresses Directly

Rahisi Agro Pay directly addresses three of these five barriers. The Learn-to-Earn module system provides modern, localised agricultural knowledge from JKUAT and KALRO in 5-minute interactive sessions. Knowledge-linked credit replaces collateral requirements. And the closed-loop Agrovet network ensures input access at the local level.

The remaining two — land and market access — represent the next phase of Oletai's product roadmap, with Group Agri-Trust scores enabling cooperative land investment, and market-linkage features planned for the 2027 platform release.

The research is clear: retain young farmers, and you retain food security. Lose them, and you inherit a crisis. Oletai was founded on the premise that technology — used correctly — is what keeps the next generation on the land.

The $4 Return: Why Investing in Climate-Smart Agriculture Is the Best Bet in East Africa

K
Kelvin Keter
CEO, Oletai Agro Group

The World Bank and Global Commission on Adaptation have calculated that the average rate of return on investments in climate resilience generates benefit-cost ratios of between 2:1 and 10:1. More specifically, an investment of $1.8 trillion in early warning systems, resilient infrastructure, and improved agriculture practices would generate $7.1 trillion in net benefits over time. Put simply: $1 in → $4 out.

"Climate change is not just an environmental challenge for East African farmers. It is their primary creditworthiness challenge. A drought doesn't just destroy crops — it destroys the farmer's financial profile for years."

The Climate-Credit Trap

Uganda's Agricultural Finance Yearbook 2022 documented a damaging cycle: climate shocks reduce agricultural yields, which reduces farmer income, which increases loan default rates, which causes banks to restrict agricultural lending further — making farmers even more vulnerable to the next climate shock. Oletai's AI Agronomist breaks this cycle at two points.

First, early detection of crop diseases and pest outbreaks — before they become harvest-destroying events — reduces the yield losses that trigger the cycle. Second, the biosurveillance data generated by Rahisi Agro Pay gives financial institutions real-time agronomic intelligence to assess and price climate risk at the farm level, rather than applying blanket sector-level risk premiums that penalise all farmers equally.

Climate Finance Is Coming to Africa — Oletai Is the Last Mile

Africa received $44 billion in climate finance in 2021/22 — a 48% increase from two years earlier. Yet recent research notes this is still far below the $250 billion annually needed. As climate finance flows increase, the critical gap is not funding — it is delivery infrastructure that reaches individual farmers.

Rahisi Agro Pay's closed-loop input voucher system ensures climate-smart seeds, drought-resistant varieties, and soil conditioners reach farmers directly — eliminating the cash leakage that often means climate finance never arrives at the farm gate.
